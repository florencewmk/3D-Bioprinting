## 10. Bioprinting technologies

Bioprinting Technologies

Source: Santoni et al. (2022), Bio-Design and Manufacturing, 5, 14–42. doi:10.1007/s42242-021-00165-0

### Speaker notes

These methods differ in how they place or solidify material.

Extrusion pushes material through a nozzle, builds three-dimensional structures by pushing a cell-containing bioink through a nozzle and depositing it layer by layer.

Inkjet printing deposits small droplets onto a receiving surface.

The droplets themselves are bioink—often a liquid hydrogel precursor containing cells

Laser-assisted transfer uses laser energy to transfer small amounts of material.

The approaches on the right use light to define where a material cures.

This is the family I’ll focus on: using light as a manufacturing tool to control a biomaterial's structure.

Two-photon polymerization belongs to this broader light-based family, although its mechanism differs from conventional SLA.

## 11. A structure forms one layer at a time

A structure forms one layer at a time

SCAN

CURE

REPEAT

Curing turns liquid bioink into a gel by forming a polymer network. Light causes selected areas of the liquid bioink to gel. We call this process curing. By controlling where it happens, we build the three-dimensional structure.

### Speaker notes

The basic idea of 3D bioprinting using light, we call as conventional stereolithography is pretty straightforward.

First, a digital model is divided into thin cross-sections.
A light source scans a pattern into a liquid material and cures selected regions.
The platform then moves, allowing fresh liquid to occupy the next printing layer.

The printed network provides a structure around those cells. However, the cells still need suitable conditions to survive, grow, and develop biological function. Such as around human temperature, nutrients and oxygen to support survival, controlled pH and steril conditions.


We repeat that sequence: scan, cure, repeat.
In bioprinting, we can use a water-rich material called a hydrogel, sometimes containing living cells. You can also of course use resin. Resin is a photo-crosslinkable liquid biomaterials. I would like to explain the word curing: Curing means turning the liquid bioink into a gel by forming a polymer network. The light causes selected areas of the liquid bioink to form a gel. We call this process curing. By controlling where it happens, we build the three-dimensional structure.



## 12. SLA · DLP · multiphoton lithography

Source: Van Ombergen et al., Advanced Healthcare Materials, 12, 2300443 (2023), Fig. 6.

A) Stereolithography (SLA)

B) Digital Light Processing (DLP)

C) Multiphoton Lithography (MPL)

### Speaker notes

These three diagrams show different ways of delivering light.
In conventional SLA, a focused laser scans the required pattern, tracing the cross-section.
In Digital Light Processing, it projects an image of a whole layer. This can reduce the time needed to expose each cross-section.
It is similar to stereolithography: both are 3D printing processes that work with photopolymers. The major difference is the light source.
DLP projects a patterned image onto the photopolymer resin, curing an entire layer in a single exposure. This generally makes it faster than laser-scanning stereolithography.

SLA scans a laser across the resin, while DLP uses an LED and a micromirror projector to expose an entire layer at once.

Multiphoton lithography focuses light into a very small volume. Under suitable conditions, curing is confined near that focus, allowing three-dimensional writing within the material.

So the distinction is: trace a pattern, project a pattern, or write within a small focal volume.

The choice affects printing speed, feature size, and the structures we can manufacture.

### Speaker notes

In conventional UV-based printing, molecules called photoinitiators absorb light and generate reactive species that start curing.
In suitable optical windows, NIR light can penetrate some biological materials more effectively than UV light.
If we use NIR light instead of UV light, we need to initiate polymerization and solidify the liquid resins.

Once the liquid thermoset resin is loaded into the vat and the digital instructions are programmed, the laser unit directs an ultraviolet beam toward a reflective mirror. From there, a galvanometer motor system steers the focused beam across the resin surface.
First, the laser draws the layers of the support structures, followed by the actual part geometry. After a layer is formed on the resin surface, an elevator system lowers the build platform. A recoater blade then moves across the platform to apply the next layer of resin.
The process repeats, layer by layer, until the build is complete.


If we use NIR light instead of UV light, we need to initiate polymerization and solidify the liquid resins.
Polymerization reaction relies on a donor-acceptor interaction: excitation, transfer, radical generation (and bleaching) -> radical is a free electron, highly reactive
Advantages: provides deeper penetration and safety, ensures fine uniformity in photocured materials, and tunable voxels (basically 3D pixels)
Disadvantages: More complex bioink formulation (requires NIR-responsive sensitizers or upconversion particles), heating risk → bioink is heated and able to damage cells
Heating must be controlled

Paulo J. Bártolo from NTU introduced a technology that combines photochemical and thermal activation.
Here, a photoinitiator absorbs two photons almost simultaneously. This becomes sufficiently likely only where the light intensity is very high, near the laser focus.
Femtosecond pulses provide that high peak intensity. By moving the focus, we can write small three-dimensional features within the material.

TPP relies on simultaneous absorption of two photons by a photoinitiator.
Occurs only at the laser focal point where photon density is high.
Excited photoinitiator generates free radicals, triggering localized polymerization.
Extremely high peak intensity: Femtosecond pulses concentrate energy in ultrashort bursts, creating the very high photon density required for two-photon absorption.
Low Average Power: Minimizes thermal diffusion, which is gentle towards the materials
Highly Localized Polymerization (Sub-Micron Resolution): Two-photon absorption only occurs at the focal point, giving extremely localized curing.
Compatibility with Near-Infrared Wavelengths: NIR has deep penetration and low absorption in tissues

## 19. Soft tissue research in orbit

Soft tissue research in orbit by NASA

BioFabrication Facility aboard the ISS / NASA

The first human knee meniscus successfully 3D bioprinted in orbit using the BioFabrication Facility


Two visual paths:
Earth → Orbit → Earth: use microgravity to investigate fabrication of delicate tissue.
Earth → Deep space: develop medical capabilities for crews far from hospitals.
Less deformation from tissue weight

Source: NASA (2023), 3D Bioprinting. nasa.gov (accessed 14 October 2026).

### Speaker notes

On Earth, soft printed materials can deform under their own weight.
But, microgravity provides a different environment for investigating the fabrication of delicate structures.

Advantage:
Less tissue collapse: In microgravity, soft printed structures deform less under their own weight. This can reduce the need for supporting scaffolds. NASA
Better research opportunities: Researchers can study how tissues form in three dimensions, potentially improving tissue models and drug testing on Earth. NASA
Future medical care for astronauts: Bioprinting could eventually provide skin or other replacement tissues during long missions far from hospitals. This remains a research goal. ESA

NASA reports the successful bioprinting of a human knee meniscus construct aboard the International Space Station using the BioFabrication Facility.
This is a broader bioprinting example, using extrusion rather than stereolithography.

It illustrates two motivations: studying tissue fabrication in microgravity, and developing medical capabilities for people far from conventional hospitals.
At the same time, space introduces challenges in handling liquids and maintaining suitable cultivation conditions.”

### Speaker notes

3D printing builds objects layer by layer
Allowing complex shapes and customized designs.
Different printing methods suit different materials and applications, including stereolithography, fused deposition modeling, and selective laser sintering.
Medical 3D printing enables patient-specific solutions, using medical images to tailor shapes to an individual’s anatomy.
Bioprinting uses living cells and biomaterials to construct tissue, which often needs time to mature after printing.
Organ shortages are a major motivation for bioprinting, with the long-term aim of producing tissues and organs for transplantation.
Clinical success requires more than printing: researchers must establish tissue function, safety, immune compatibility, regulation, and fair access.

## 30. Ultraviolet photopolymerization

Ultraviolet photopolymerization

Mechanism: UV light activates photoinitiators → radicals → curing
Strength: Established printing with fine detail
Tradeoff: Limited penetration and potential cell damage

### Speaker notes

Let’s look at conventional UV photopolymerization more closely.

Once the liquid thermoset resin is loaded into the vat and the digital instructions are programmed, the laser unit directs an ultraviolet beam toward a reflective mirror. From there, a galvanometer motor system steers the focused beam across the resin surface.
First, the laser draws the layers of the support structures, followed by the actual part geometry. After a layer is formed on the resin surface, an elevator system lowers the build platform. A recoater blade then moves across the platform to apply the next layer of resin.
The process repeats, layer by layer, until the build is complete.


The liquid contains building blocks that can form a polymer network, together with a photoinitiator.
When the photoinitiator absorbs suitable light, it generates radicals. These reactive species initiate reactions between the building blocks, forming a connected network.
Hydrogel holds water and gives the printed structure its shape.
The printing system controls where this reaction happens through the light pattern and exposure.
If the exposure is too low, the material may be insufficiently cured. If it is too high, curing can spread beyond the intended region and reduce accuracy.
For cell-containing materials, there is another constraint: excessive light exposure, radicals, or unsuitable initiator concentrations can harm cells.
So we are balancing three things at once: structural accuracy, sufficient curing, and biological compatibility.

Photoinitiators have strong absorption peaks in UV
Energy is high enough to efficiently break chemical bonds
UV shrinks the beam spot → high resolution
UV (especially 355 nm) →  smaller spot size →  finer details
405 nm →  cheaper + still works with modern photoinitiators
Advantages: Simple system, large resin compatibility, lower material cost, mature, widely used, predictable
Disadvantages: Limited penetration, portrays high phototoxicity, damages the cellular DNA, reduces viability, incomplete cure  → post-processing required



build platform above vat window, thin film fills gap, gap height=one layer thickness
NIR laser scans across resin layer, traces 2D cross-section pattern, only scanned areas receives light
Solidification: Phototchemical (Heptamethine cyanine and Upconversion Nanoparticle)
Build platform descends, creates gap for next year
Liquid resin flows into new gap, forms uniform liquid ready for next exposure
Repeat step 2-5
Platform raises out of vat
Minimizes Resin Consumption: The vat only needs to be deep enough to cover the part and allow for re-coating, which is efficient for expensive UCNP-doped resins.
Eliminates Deep Penetration Requirements for Layering: Since each layer is cured at the window interface, the NIR laser only needs to penetrate a short distance (tens of microns) to cure the next layer, ensuring high resolution and consistent curing energy. This avoids the problem of the beam diverging and losing focus over long distances.




## 31. Near-infrared photopolymerization

Near-infrared photopolymerization

Mechanism: NIR-responsive chemistry generates radicals
Strength: Greater optical penetration
Tradeoff: More complex bioinks and possible heating

### Speaker notes

If we use NIR light instead of UV light, we need to initiate polymerization and solidify the liquid resins.
Polymerization reaction relies on a donor-acceptor interaction: excitation, transfer, radical generation (and bleaching) -> radical is a free electron, highly reactive
Advantages: provides deeper penetration and safety, ensures fine uniformity in photocured materials, and tunable voxels (basically 3D pixels)
Disadvantages: More complex bioink formulation (requires NIR-responsive sensitizers or upconversion particles), heating risk → bioink is heated and able to damage cells
Heating must be controlled


With near-infrared photopolymerization, the final goal is still to generate reactive species that form the polymer network.
What changes is how we get there.
The first route shown here uses a cyanine sensitizer.
The cyanine absorbs NIR light and becomes excited. It can then transfer an electron to a co-initiator, such as an iodonium salt. Subsequent reactions generate radicals that initiate polymerization.
A second route uses upconversion nanoparticles.
These particles absorb NIR excitation and emit shorter-wavelength light, which can activate a suitable photoinitiating system.
Both routes allow us to use NIR excitation, but they require additional components in the formulation.
That introduces practical questions: are the components well dispersed? Do they absorb efficiently? Are they compatible with cells?

## 33. Beam Quality & Spot Size

Beam Quality & Spot Size

Beam Quality (M² Factor)
M² describes how close the beam is to a perfect Gaussian.
Lower M² → tighter focus & better accuracy.

Why Beam Quality Matters
A high-quality beam maintains its shape while scanning,
giving sharper edges and finer details.
Poor beam quality increases divergence, making the laser spot spread out.

Spot Size Limitations
Spot size depends on wavelength, optics, and M².
Shorter wavelengths (355 nm) + low M², smallest possible spot.
Smaller spot = higher resolution but slower because scanning covers less area.

### Speaker notes

“Beam quality plays a huge role in SLA resolution.
It is measured using the M-squared value, where an M² of 1 represents a perfect Gaussian beam.
Typical UV DPSS lasers have M² values between 1.1 and 1.7, which means they can be focused very tightly.
A better beam quality allows a smaller laser spot size, sharper curing, and higher printing resolution.
In contrast, multimode lasers with higher M² values produce larger spots and less precision.”

## 34. Conventional SLA Mechanism

Conventional SLA Mechanism

Type I Mechanism:
Cleavage (Fast)

UV photon absorption → α-cleavage of the photoinitiator (Norrish Type I).
Generates two radicals instantly, giving high radical concentration.
Does not require co-initiators, improving reaction purity and speed.
Supports rapid curing, deeper light penetration, and strong mechanical properties.

Type II Mechanism:
H-Abstraction (Slower)

Excited photoinitiator must collide with an amine co-initiator.
Hydrogen abstraction creates two radicals, but reaction is bimolecular, so slower.
Camphorquinone (CQ) has weak absorption at 405 nm, reducing efficiency.
Produces amine-based byproducts → yellowing and slower polymerization.

### Speaker notes

“Conventional SLA relies entirely on single-photon polymerization.
For Type I photoinitiators, the mechanism is simple:
the molecule absorbs one UV photon and immediately splits into two radicals.
These radicals start chain-growth polymerization almost instantly.
For Type II, the initiator first absorbs light and then interacts with a co-initiator, usually an amine, to generate radicals.
Because this system has an extra reaction step, it tends to be slower and less efficient than Type I.”

## 41. Working principle of STLG

Working principle of STLG

Irradiation process in STLG technique

In multi-vat system

### Speaker notes

The system consists of power supply, light split, collimating probes, UV filters, two digital micromirror devices (DMDs) with each employed with UV and IR beams, a dichroic mirror and a polymetric system.
The DMD-UV and DMD-IR able to pattern the UV and IR beams independently, allowing more control of the light wavelength for where it should be delivered.
A dichroic mirror is used to superimpose the two patterned beams before projecting them onto the resin.
The dichroic mirror reflects one wavelength at a time and transmits another to keep the spatial overlapping precise.
Finally, both the UV and IR patterned beams are projected onto a photopolymerizable resin. STLG triggers polymerization only where both energies intersect, thus achieving high localization and efficiency.

Automated STLG work cell designed for multimaterial fabrication.
The work cell consists of a rotating platform with six resin vats in it, each containing different resin.
Only one resin is active under the light focus.
Two DMDs are used to program patterns, one using UV and another using IR light.
Each of the DMDs includes collimating probes and connected to power supplies.
An optical system is introduced to superimpose the patterned UV and IR images where it focuses both radiations onto a single spot in the active resin vat.
The entire work cell can rotate and allows automated switching between different resins, thus enables multi-material printing at once.

## 44. Beyond STLG: Photothermal Conversion Mechanism

Beyond STLG: Photothermal Conversion Mechanism

High-resolution photothermal stereolithography bioprinting remains a challenge since confining heat in an aqueous environment is difficult as the heat dissipates too quickly

Imaging-Guided Microscale Photothermal Stereolithography Bioprinting (ImPSB)

### Speaker notes

The researchers aimed to achieve high-resolution aqueous bioprinting yet low cost by using thermal initiators (TIs) instead of conventional photoinitiators (PIs).
The difference between ImPSB and STLG are that in ImPSB aimed to achieve microscale resolution and cellular biocompatibility in aqueous photothermal SLA bioprinting while in the STLG method the goal was to improve accuracy, speed and quality of macroscopic engineering prototypes by achieving a higher quality of cure and avoiding post-curing.
Jingyu Sun et al. engineered a layered gold-platinum (LAuPt) nanoframework as photothermal agents in the bioink.
When exposed to NIR-II light (1100–1500 nm) for printing, these nanoframeworks absorb the NIR energy and convert it into localized heat.
Firstly, The LAuPt framework was loaded with a thermal initiator. The 2,2′-azobis [2- (2-imidazolin-2-yl) propane]-dihydrochloride (AIPH) aqueous solutions was used as the thermal initiator.
When exposed to NIR-II irradiation, the LAuPt absorbs the light and temperature rises rapidly.
The surrounding medium was locally heated. Next, the generated heat decomposes the AIPH inside the nanoframework, thus producing free alkyl radicals.
These radicals then initiate the polymerization of polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) in the bioink, transforming it from liquid to solid hydrogel at targeted locations.
The photothermal effect is confined to the illuminated focal volume by the NIR laser. As a result, the gelation is highly localized.
Jingyu Sun et al. were able to achieve microscale and biocompatible realization using the proposed light- and heat polymerization STLG method.
This method makes polymerization possible in the absences of UV light or high photoinitiators concentrations and results in improved cellular compatibility for bioprinting.
Therefore, ImPSB is an enhanced and developed from STLG by using NIR-II light instead of IR radiation.
