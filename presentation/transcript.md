## 09. Bioink and curing

Two words to know

Source: Li et al., iScience, 26, 106039 (2023)

### Speaker notes

For bioprinting, there are two important keywords.

First, the bioink. Bioink is basically just like the ink in a normal printer, but with cells.

Second, curing, which is the printing process. When the light hits the photoinitiator, a chemical compound, it splits into free radicals. Because they’re very reactive, they eventually link together into chains. Curing means turning the liquid bioink into a gel by forming this polymer network. The light makes selected areas of the liquid bioink turn into a gel. By controlling where that happens, we build the three-dimensional structure.

### Speaker notes

To bioprint, first you’ll need a small biopsy sample from the patient. Then we bring it to the lab to grow more cells from it, mix them into the bioink, and finally print it with light.

After printing, we need to take care of its growing environment. When it has matured, it can go back to the patient.

Theoretically, the patient is less likely to reject the new organ, since it’s grown from their own cells.

## 11. Two ways to build

Two ways to build
Light from below, or from above
Bottom-up
Light shines up through a clear window in the tank floor. Each new layer sticks to that window, so the platform lifts and peels it off before the next layer.
+ Needs only a shallow pool of bioink
– Peeling pulls on the freshly printed bioink
Top-down
The classic set-up: the laser sits above and cures the surface. The platform sinks deeper and a blade spreads fresh liquid.
+ No peeling, gentle on the soft bioink
– The tank must be full: more bioink
Source: Li et al., iScience, 26, 106039 (2023)

### Speaker notes

There are two ways to print it: with light from below or from above. We call them the bottom-up and the top-down method.

For bottom-up, the light shines from below through a clear window, and each layer hardens against that window. So it sticks there a bit like a fresh pancake to a pan. Before the next layer, the platform has to pull the part up and peel it off the window. That pull is the peeling force. For a hard material like plastic it’s OK, but a soft, jelly-like bioink can tear or bend.

For top-down, the light scans from above, and the platform moves down. The downside is that the vat has to be big enough for the object you’re printing.

### Speaker notes

The light used here is usually UV, near-infrared, or both at the same time, which we call stereo-thermal lithography.

UV interacts strongly with tissue, so the cells can get damaged easily.

Near-infrared light penetrates deeper and is therefore gentler on cells, but it needs special photoinitiators to work.

Stereo-thermal lithography uses both at the same time, with heat generated by the NIR light. The downside is that the heat must be controlled so it doesn’t damage the cells.

## 13. What is still in the way

What’s still in the way?
Fine detail or big size
Multiphoton printing reaches sub-micrometre detail, but only a few mm³ per hour. Faster methods are coarser.
Laws lag behind
Regulators still struggle to classify printed tissue, and it does not fit today’s clinical-trial rules.
Room for misuse
Without joint regulation, a new black market in printed organs could appear.
Keeping cells alive
The biggest hurdle: without blood vessels, tissue cannot grow beyond 100–200 µm.
Source: Van Ombergen et al., Adv. Healthcare Mater., 12, 2300443 (2023) · Vermeulen et al., J. Med. Ethics, 43, 618–624 (2017) · Bishop et al., Genes & Diseases, 4, 185–195 (2017)

### Speaker notes

The biggest challenge for bioprinting is to print with high resolution and accuracy.

Not to mention, right now there is no law about bioprinting. Authorities will have to make legal decisions on that.

We also have to consider that people may misuse this technology and sell it on the black market.

Last but not least, it is also very challenging to keep the cells alive. Why?

Source: Bishop et al., Genes & Diseases, 4, 185–195 (2017) · Asakura, J. Nippon Med. Sch., 71, 360–370 (2004)

### Speaker notes

Think of it as a baby in the mother’s womb. What does the baby need while it’s still in the tummy? Of course, it needs oxygen, enough nutrients, a suitable temperature, a suitable pH level and sterile conditions.

The problem with bioprinting is that the cells don’t get enough oxygen, since there are no blood vessels transporting the oxygen molecules.

Think of being in a small, crowded room. The people near the window get fresh air, but the people in the middle are stuck and suffocate. That’s exactly the situation for bioprinted cells in the incubator.

## 15. Where are we today?

Where are we today?
In the lab
Printed blood vessels and a mini heart
Harvard’s Wyss Institute prints branching vessels inside living heart tissue (2024). Tel Aviv printed a heart the size of a rabbit’s from a patient’s own cells (2019). It cannot pump yet.
In a patient
A printed ear
In 2022, a woman born with microtia received an ear printed from her own cartilage cells. It was the first clinical trial of its kind.
In space
Printing on the space station
NASA’s BioFabrication Facility printed a human knee meniscus on the ISS (2023). Germany tested a handheld bio-plaster printer there (2022).
In medicine
Ready for hospitals?
Not yet. Maybe in the next 10 years, starting with simple tissues like skin and cartilage. Whole organs will take longer.
Source: Harvard SEAS / Wyss Institute (2024) · Tel Aviv University (2019) · CELLINK / 3DBio (2022) · NASA (2023) · DLR (2022)

### Speaker notes

Right now, there are already many scientists and research groups working on this technology.

Harvard, for example, printed blood vessels, and Tel Aviv University printed a heart.

A successful example: a group in the US printed an ear for a patient using the patient’s own cells.

NASA also printed a human meniscus in space, aiming to benefit their astronauts on deep-space missions in the future. And Germany printed a bio-plaster. It’s not close to printing organs, but in a medical sense it’s also useful.

Bioprinting is still a new technology and not ready for hospital use. Maybe in the next 10 years, or even longer for a functional organ.

### Speaker notes

These three diagrams show different ways of delivering light.

Spot Size Limitations
Spot size depends on wavelength, optics, and M².
Shorter wavelengths (355 nm) + low M², smallest possible spot.

## 41. Conventional SLA Mechanism

Conventional SLA Mechanism

Type I Mechanism:
Cleavage (Fast)

UV photon absorption → α-cleavage of the photoinitiator (Norrish Type I).
Generates two radicals instantly, giving high radical concentration.
Does not require co-initiators, improving reaction purity and speed.
Supports rapid curing, deeper light penetration, and strong mechanical properties.

Type II Mechanism:
H-Abstraction (Slower)

Excited photoinitiator must collide with an amine co-initiator.
Hydrogen abstraction creates two radicals, but reaction is bimolecular, so slower.
Camphorquinone (CQ) has weak absorption at 405 nm, reducing efficiency.
Produces amine-based byproducts → yellowing and slower polymerization.

### Speaker notes

“Conventional SLA relies entirely on single-photon polymerization.
For Type I photoinitiators, the mechanism is simple:
the molecule absorbs one UV photon and immediately splits into two radicals.
These radicals start chain-growth polymerization almost instantly.
For Type II, the initiator first absorbs light and then interacts with a co-initiator, usually an amine, to generate radicals.
Because this system has an extra reaction step, it tends to be slower and less efficient than Type I.”

## 51. Beyond STLG: Photothermal Conversion Mechanism

Beyond STLG: Photothermal Conversion Mechanism

High-resolution photothermal stereolithography bioprinting remains a challenge since confining heat in an aqueous environment is difficult as the heat dissipates too quickly

Imaging-Guided Microscale Photothermal Stereolithography Bioprinting (ImPSB)

### Speaker notes

The researchers aimed to achieve high-resolution aqueous bioprinting yet low cost by using thermal initiators (TIs) instead of conventional photoinitiators (PIs).
The difference between ImPSB and STLG are that in ImPSB aimed to achieve microscale resolution and cellular biocompatibility in aqueous photothermal SLA bioprinting while in the STLG method the goal was to improve accuracy, speed and quality of macroscopic engineering prototypes by achieving a higher quality of cure and avoiding post-curing.
Jingyu Sun et al. engineered a layered gold-platinum (LAuPt) nanoframework as photothermal agents in the bioink.
When exposed to NIR-II light (1100–1500 nm) for printing, these nanoframeworks absorb the NIR energy and convert it into localized heat.
Firstly, The LAuPt framework was loaded with a thermal initiator. The 2,2′-azobis [2- (2-imidazolin-2-yl) propane]-dihydrochloride (AIPH) aqueous solutions was used as the thermal initiator.
When exposed to NIR-II irradiation, the LAuPt absorbs the light and temperature rises rapidly.
The surrounding medium was locally heated. Next, the generated heat decomposes the AIPH inside the nanoframework, thus producing free alkyl radicals.
These radicals then initiate the polymerization of polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) in the bioink, transforming it from liquid to solid hydrogel at targeted locations.
The photothermal effect is confined to the illuminated focal volume by the NIR laser. As a result, the gelation is highly localized.
Jingyu Sun et al. were able to achieve microscale and biocompatible realization using the proposed light- and heat polymerization STLG method.
This method makes polymerization possible in the absences of UV light or high photoinitiators concentrations and results in improved cellular compatibility for bioprinting.
Therefore, ImPSB is an enhanced and developed from STLG by using NIR-II light instead of IR radiation.
